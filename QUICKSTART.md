# Your first decision memo in 30 minutes

The fastest way to find out whether this repo is useful to you is to write one memo for one real decision that is currently stuck. Not a template exercise. A decision your team has been circling for more than a week.

You need: the [decision memo template](templates/decision-memo.md), the [worked example](examples/decision-memo-example.md) open beside it, and thirty minutes without a meeting.

## Minutes 0 to 5: pick the decision

Choose the question your team has discussed most and decided least. The signs: a thread that keeps reviving, a topic that appears on three consecutive agendas, or a sentence people say with a sigh ("we still need to figure out the pricing page").

Write it as a question with a yes/no or A/B answer. If you cannot, you have found the first problem: the team has been discussing a topic, not a decision. Narrow it until it has an answer.

> Weak: "What should we do about the onboarding flow?"
> Usable: "Do we replace the five-step onboarding with the two-step version before the spring campaign?"

## Minutes 5 to 10: owner and date

One name. If the honest answer is "the leadership team" or "product and engineering together," pick the person who would be blamed if it went wrong. That is the owner.

Then a date. Pick the date by which not deciding starts to cost something. If nothing happens if you do not decide, the decision may not need to be made at all. That is a legitimate finding; write it down and stop.

## Minutes 10 to 20: the options table

Three rows. Options A and B are the ones people have been arguing for. Row C is always "do nothing," described honestly: what it costs to keep things as they are. Most teams have never written that row down, and it is often the most clarifying thing in the memo.

For each row, fill in the three columns: what it gets us, what it costs, what it rules out. Keep every cell to one line. If you need a paragraph, the option is not yet understood well enough to decide on.

Do not write the recommendation yet.

## Minutes 20 to 25: what would change our mind

Skip the recommendation and write this section first. One or two facts that, if they turned out differently, would flip the choice. This is the section that stops the decision from being remade in three months, because the people who disagreed know exactly what to watch.

If you cannot think of anything that would change your mind, you are not making a decision. You are announcing one. That can be fine, but then the memo is a communication, and it should say so.

## Minutes 25 to 30: recommendation and what this stops

Now the recommendation: which option and why, in three sentences or fewer. Then the hardest line in the memo: what stops if we choose this. Not "deprioritized." Stops. If the answer is "nothing," your capacity just shrank by one initiative and nobody has said so.

Leave "who was consulted" blank. Fill it in by sending the memo to the two or three people who disagree most and asking each for one line on where they land. Disagreement recorded in the memo is cheaper than disagreement discovered after the decision.

## What you have after 30 minutes

A one-page memo with an owner, a date, honest options and a tripwire. Send it to the owner. The decision will often happen within the week, because most slow decisions were never slow; they were unowned.

Then log it: one row in the [decision log](decision-velocity/decision-log.md). After ten rows, read the [example log](examples/decision-log-example.md) to see what the pattern tells you.

## If it did not work

- **The memo ran to three pages.** You have two or three decisions in one. Split it.
- **Nobody would own it.** The decision sits above the team. Send the memo up with the owner line blank and the date filled in; that is a decision request, and it is already more than most teams send.
- **The owner decided without reading it.** Good. Log it anyway. The point was the decision, not the memo.
